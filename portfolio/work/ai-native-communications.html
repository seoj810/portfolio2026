01Setup

Context and stakes.

The project. An internal exploration into what communications could look like when AI is at the core — what happens to calling, messaging, and presence in a world where AI is native, not added on.

My scope. Just two designers, covering the entire comms surface: messaging, calling, contacts, presence. No brief beyond the core question. Since this was an exploration sprint, all constraints were intentionally lifted.

02Problem definition

The brief was the problem.

The frame I was handed assumed a destination — but the more useful work was reframing the question.

How do we add AI to our communications apps?
What does communication look like when AI is first-class — and when it’s no longer organized around apps?

Before designing anything, I had to understand what communication fundamentally is underneath the app layer. What is a phone call? What job is a user trying to do — before apps were the answer to that job?

03Approach

Concepts, not screens.

i.

Deconstructed the fundamental intent

What calling and messaging exist to do for users, stripped of any product layer.

ii.

Mapped the behavioral shift required

Moving from “open an app” to a free-flowing conversation with an AI. No chrome. No guidance. No familiar UI anchors.

iii.

Made the primary artifact a Google Doc

Working in screens too early anchors you to the wrong level of abstraction. Systems before surfaces.

04Framework

Four levels of AI participation.

The result was a framework that named the design space — a way to talk about AI’s role in communication that wasn’t just “add AI features.”

Level 01

Direct Connection

AI as transparent pipe — zero interference. “Call Mom on WhatsApp.” The assistant dials immediately and gets out of the way.

Level 02

Background Assist

AI as passive context layer — never interrupts. In a group call, the agent cross-references calendars silently and surfaces a “best dates” summary.

Level 03

Negotiated Handoff

AI handles friction — hands off for the live moment. The agent navigates hold music and transfers. You join when the right person is already on the line.

Most traction
Level 04

Full Delegation

AI handles everything — notifies after completion. “Get my flight refunded.” The agent calls the airline, handles the rep, pings you when it’s done.

05Diving deeper

Full Delegation isn’t one thing.

Level 04 had the most organizational pull — but it isn’t a single design problem. The agent might be talking to a person, or to another agent. Those are fundamentally different surfaces.

04A

Agent × Human

The agent interacts with a person on the other end. It must navigate tone, social etiquette, emotional cues, reading the room — all the unwritten contracts of human communication.

04B — Explored further

Agent × Agent

The agent interacts with another AI — an airline’s booking system, a scheduling assistant. No social friction. Entirely transactional. But new questions: what protocols govern this? What does trust look like between two AIs?

Agent-to-agent communication isn’t an interface problem — it’s a protocol problem, a trust problem, and an experience design problem simultaneously.

06Prototype

Ended the sprint with a prototype.

The sprint ended with a prototype: a high-level look at what agents do in the background on a user’s behalf, and how that gets surfaced back to them.

View prototype →
Key design questions
What scenarios are agents most powerful in? How do agents know what’s shareable vs. private? When is human consent necessary? How does the user course-correct if the agent is wrong? Should the agent have its own voice and identity?
07Outcome

Vocabulary and direction.

This didn’t ship. That’s the honest answer. This was an exploration; the value was never going to be a launched product, and I want to name that directly. What it produced was direction.

Org impact
Shared vocabulary for AI’s role in comms
Seeded
Active agent-to-agent workstream
Status
Exploration · 2026

The framework gave teams a way to talk about AI’s role that wasn’t just “add AI features” — it named the design space. The agent-to-agent distinction got picked up and became an active prototype investment area. The exploration gave the org a direction it could act on, which is what good exploratory design should produce.

08Reflection

The hard questions still open.

i.

Relationships are complex

Agents need to map the nuances of how people relate to one another — not just who you’re talking to, but how. A much harder modeling problem than a contact list.

ii.

Social intelligence

Etiquette, emotional cues, reading the room. Are our agents intelligent enough to factor these in? If they’re not, we need to design around that gap — not past it.

iii.

Safety and privacy

For any of this to work at scale, users have to trust the agent isn’t doing more than they sanctioned. That’s not a legal checkbox — it’s a design challenge.

iv.

The social profile problem

A profile may not be a simple list. To truly capture someone’s social comfort, it may need to be far more complex and dynamic than anything we’ve designed before.

What does it actually mean to represent someone’s relationships faithfully enough for an agent to act on their behalf?
